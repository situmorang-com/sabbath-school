Q3 2026 · 1 & 2 Corinthians · Lesson 9

Love-driven Ministry

A Sabbath School teacher’s guide, built on this week’s official lesson.

  • Sabbath29 August 2026
  • Time40 minutes
  • ClassAdults, mixed
Lesson week
22–28 August 2026, discussed in class on Sabbath 29 August
Read for This Week
1:3-14; 2:17; 4:2; 16:5-7; 7:5-13; 2:5-17
Key text the class leaves holding
2 Corinthians 2:8: “Wherefore I beseech you that ye would confirm your love toward him.”

“For out of much affliction and anguish of heart I wrote unto you with many tears; not that ye should be grieved, but that ye might know the love which I have more abundantly unto you.”

2 Corinthians 2:4 (KJV)

1 What This Week Is Actually Arguing

The title sounds warm. “Love-driven Ministry” reads like a devotional about being kind. That is not what is here. This week is Paul defending himself before a church that has accused him of insincerity.

The charge is plain in the text itself. Paul is called changeable, a man who says yes and no in the same breath (1:17). He is accused of hidden motives (1:12). Some in Corinth put him in the same class as preachers who peddle the word of God for profit (2:17). Their evidence is simple and looks strong: Paul promised to come, and did not come.

Paul’s answer is the reversal that carries the whole week. The very things that read as less love were the form his love took. He stayed away because a second visit would add grief, not joy (1:23-24; 2:1). He wrote the painful letter not to grieve them but so they would know how abundantly he loved them (2:4). Love in this chapter looks like absence. Love in this chapter looks like a letter that made people cry.

What the lesson is not saying: that good intentions are always enough, or that anyone who feels hurt must be misreading. Paul never says “you are too sensitive.” He explains himself at length, for two full chapters, because he judged the misunderstanding worth answering rather than ignoring.

Where the week is heading: not to Paul’s vindication, but to the command in 2:8. After all that explaining, what Paul asks of the church is not an apology to him. It is that they confirm their love toward the man who hurt them. A week that opens with an apostle defending himself closes with an apostle telling a church to forgive somebody else.

2 Objectives

  • Know

    That 2 Corinthians 1 and 2 are Paul’s answer to a charge of insincerity, and that the changed travel plans and the severe letter were the shape of his love, not its absence.

  • Feel

    The double discomfort: having loved someone in a way they read as rejection, and having misread someone else exactly that way.

  • Do

    Name, silently, one person whose motives you judged without knowing their reasons, and one person currently under the church’s displeasure. Before next Sabbath, contact one of the two.

That last one is concrete enough to be declined. Someone will say they cannot face the second person. That is an honest answer, and more useful than a nod.

3 Discussion Plan, 40 Minutes

Opening, minutes 0–3

Do not start with Paul. Start with a question anyone can answer without preparation:

“Has anyone ever tried to comfort you and made you feel worse? What did they say?”

“Do not worry; this was part of God’s plan.” Heard that one at a funeral?

Two or three answers, then close with the week’s claim:

“This week Paul is accused of not loving his church. The evidence: he promised to come and did not come. His answer: it was precisely because I loved you that I stayed away.”

Then pray. Do not summarize the lesson in the prayer.

Sunday: Thanksgiving

23 August · minutes 26–32

1:3-11

What this day is doing

Laying the foundation. Before Paul defends himself, he establishes that he has standing to speak about suffering, because he has just come through it.

Points to hold

  • The verb parakaleō (to comfort) and the noun paraklēsis (comfort) occur ten times in 1:3-7 alone. That is one third of all their occurrences in 2 Corinthians. The lesson gives this figure, and it is worth saying aloud: repetition on that scale is not style, it is emphasis.
  • Comfort travels one way and is then passed on. We are comforted so that we are able to comfort (1:4). It is not comfort to be kept.
  • In 1:9 Paul says they had the sentence of death in themselves, and he gives the reason himself: that they should not trust in themselves. The suffering has a stated purpose, and the class does not have to guess at it.

Quotes for this day

“It is your privilege to receive grace from Christ that will enable you to comfort others with the same comfort wherewith you yourselves are comforted of God.... Let each try to help the next one.”

God’s Amazing Grace, p. 122.4. Open this paragraph

Use: this is 2 Corinthians 1:4 in other words, on the same page as the Friday quotation, and the lesson does not print it. Read it at 26–32 as the closing line for Sunday, stopping after “help the next one.”

the Greek behind “comforteth” implies a comfort continued without interruption, and coming through the agency of the Holy Spirit.

Use: answers the assumption that God’s comfort is a single event at the moment of crisis.

“Sentence” is literally “answer.” The tense of the Greek verb implies that the vivid recollection of the near-death experience still seemed real as Paul wrote.

Use: shows 1:8-9 is not figurative language. It is not evidence for which event was meant; SDABC itself notes several proposals.

“Paul is not writing about comfort from a quiet study. He had just expected to die, and he tells us the reason: so that he would stop leaning on himself.”

Spare question

“Why do you think the people who have suffered most are often the ones best able to comfort, when by rights they should be the most worn out?”

Monday: Simplicity and Sincerity

24 August · minutes 26–32

1:12-14; 2:17; 4:2

What this day is doing

Setting the thesis of the letter. 1:12-14 is the sentence Paul will spend the rest of the letter proving.

Points to hold

  • Two words carry the day in the KJV: simplicity and godly sincerity (1:12). There is a genuine textual question in the first of them, and it explains why two official documents on your desk read differently. See Section 6, item 1.
  • Paul does not claim these qualities as his own achievement. In the same verse he grounds them in God and “by the grace of God.”
  • Set 2:17 and 4:2 beside it. All three use the same register and together form one portrait: not corrupting the word of God, not walking in craftiness, not handling it deceitfully.

Quotes for this day

Three below. Take the SDABC first; keep Ellen G. White in reserve for a member who raises the question of conscience.

here Paul begins discussing his recent relations with the church at Corinth. He has just claimed the right to their intercessory prayers (v. 11), and now declares he has not forfeited that claim by his conduct. Some of the Corinthians had charged him with insincere intentions regarding his change of plans. His conscience was void of offense before God and before them.

Use: connects Monday directly to Tuesday. The charge is not about doctrine, it is about a travel schedule. One sentence at 26–32.

“Many” is literally “the many,” that is, those who opposed Paul. Apparently a large number of church members in Corinth had concluded that “the many” could not be wrong. To them the only important question was: which is the popular side?

Use: sharp and easy to say aloud, but it can start a discussion of its own. Use only if you have time in hand.

“The greatest disquietude we can have is uncertainty.... We must have simplicity and Godlike sincerity.”

The Upward Look, p. 99. Already printed on Monday’s lesson page

Use: lands on the day’s key words directly. Reserve only; Monday has no block of its own.

“The charge against Paul is not about his teaching. It is about his character: he said he would come, he did not come, so his word cannot be trusted. This whole letter is the answer to that charge.”

Spare question

“When were your motives last questioned, when you knew your intention was good? What did you do?”

Tuesday: Changing Plans for Love

25 August · minutes 6–16, the weight-bearing day

16:5-7; 1:15-24; 2:1-4

What this day is doing

This is the centre of the week. Here the reversal happens: what read as indifference turns out to be the form the care took.

This is the weight-bearing day. Read 1:15-24 and 2:1-4 aloud from the Bible, not from the lesson’s summary. Fourteen verses takes about two minutes. Leave eight minutes for questions.

Points to hold

  • Paul’s plans changed twice, not once. The original in 16:5-6 was Macedonia then Corinth. The second, in 1:15-16, was Ephesus, Corinth, Macedonia, Corinth, Judea. What happened: the visit went badly, he returned to Ephesus, and he wrote instead of making a third trip.
  • Paul’s defense does not rest on himself. In 1:18-20 he moves their eyes to Christ: all the promises of God are yea and amen in Him. The argument is that the gospel he preached can be relied on, and therefore so can he.
  • 1:24 is easy to miss and very strong: “not for that we have dominion over your faith.” Paul has just exercised apostolic authority, and immediately limits it himself.
  • Notice the order. Paul defends himself only after speaking at length about suffering and comfort: the suffering lays the foundation for the claim of sincerity. A man who has lost that much is not in it for profit. (Cameron, ssnet)

Quotes for this day

“With ‘anguish of heart’ and with ‘many tears’ he sought counsel from God. Gladly would he have visited Corinth at once, had this been the wisest course to pursue. But he knew that in their present condition the believers would not profit by his labors, and therefore he sent Titus to prepare the way for a visit from himself later on.”

The Acts of the Apostles, pp. 300, 301. Already printed on Tuesday’s lesson page. Open the book

Use: the strongest quotation of the week, and it belongs here, not on Friday. Read it at minute eight or nine, right after somebody asks why Paul did not simply go, then stop after “later on.” Ellen G. White uses the Memory Text’s own phrases, which hands the class a sentence that is hard to argue with: Paul’s decision came out of prayer, not avoidance.

God’s promises are dependable.

Use: be honest with the class that the commentary on a verse this large turns out to be one line. The verse does the work here, not the note on it.

“Sealed” is sphragizō, to mark with a seal, to authenticate, to confirm. A seal attests the genuineness of the document it is affixed to. The seal God places on men and women acknowledges them as His sons and daughters, established in Christ and dedicated to His service.

Use: if someone pulls vv. 21-22 toward the seal of God in Revelation, this keeps the discussion on the text. SDABC does cross-reference Revelation 7:2-3 and 14:1, so the link is fair to mention, but it is not what Paul is discussing here.

Spare question

“If Paul really was led by God when he made the first plan, why did the plan change?”

Wednesday: Forgiveness and Reaffirmation of Love

26 August · minutes 16–26, the second real question

7:5-13; 2:5-11

What this day is doing

This is where the cost is paid. For two days the class has been defending Paul. Now Paul turns and demands something of them, toward somebody else.

Read 2:5-11. Seven verses. Then one question, and be quiet. Four to six seconds of silence after that question is the question working.

Points to hold

  • The command in 2:8 is not merely “forgive him.” The KJV has “confirm your love toward him.” That is an action other people can see, not an inward posture nobody can check.
  • The purpose of the discipline is stated in 2:7: lest he be swallowed up with overmuch sorrow. At this stage the danger Paul is worried about is no longer the man’s sin. It is a church that will not stop punishing.
  • 2:11 gives a surprising reason: lest Satan should get an advantage. So there are two ways for the church to lose, not one. See Section 6, item 3.
  • The lesson notes that scholars differ on whether this man is the offender of 5:1-5, and the lesson leans toward a different person. SDABC leans the other way. Say the disagreement plainly; do not pick one and hide the other.

Quotes for this day

Four sources for this day, more than any other. Take SDABC on 2:6 first, it is the most directly usable. Keep the rest for questions that come up.

the objective of church discipline had been accomplished; the offender had repented, and it was time to restore him to the confidence and fellowship of his brethren. Christian discipline is a work of love, not of vengeance. Its objective is not revenge but restoration. It must uphold the commandments of God and maintain church order, safeguard the other members and protect the good name of the church, but it should also, wherever possible, lead the sinner to repentance.

Use: holds both camps that will appear in your class without handing either one the win. Read it after the discussion has started to split, around minute 22, not before.

having made an incision and accomplished his purpose, a surgeon binds up the wound and seeks to restore his patient to health. The offender at Corinth had been deprived of Christian fellowship. But now that he had repented, further discipline would be vindictive and punitive, and would tend to discourage him from being loyal to his new resolve.

Use: the surgeon image answers the objection that forgiving too quickly makes sin look cheap. One sentence, then back to the text.

“Give Satan No Advantage—In the conflict with satanic agencies there are decisive moments that determine the victory either on the side of God or on the side of the prince of this world.”

SDA Bible Commentary, vol. 6, p. 1094.2, commenting on 2 Corinthians 2:11. Open this paragraph

Use: shifts the register from a debate about procedure to a matter of the great controversy. Use it around minute 24 if the class gets stuck on process.

godly sorrow is not sorrow at being found out or in anticipation of punishment. It is genuine sorrow for sin, repentance of it, separation from it, and determination to resist the temptation that led to it. Embarrassment at exposure, fear of exposure, injured pride, even profound regret over what has happened: none of this is godly sorrow. The supreme illustration is the contrast between Peter and Judas during the trial of Jesus. Both felt bitter remorse, but with one it led to a new life in Christ, with the other to utter despair.

Use: Peter and Judas is the fastest way to make 7:10 concrete. Save it for the application at 32–38 if someone brings an old regret into the room.

Spare question

“When someone under church discipline repents, whose job is it to make the first move toward repairing the relationship: theirs, or ours?”

Thursday: Triumph in Christ

27 August · minutes 26–32, then used again in the closing

2:12-17

What this day is doing

Closing the story and lifting it. Titus finally arrives, the news is good, and Paul breaks into thanksgiving. Then he gives the image that carries the whole week home.

Points to hold

  • 2:12-13 is honest in a way that is rarely noticed: a door was opened at Troas, and Paul left anyway, because he had no rest in his spirit waiting for news. This is the only record of Paul turning away from an open door.
  • The “savour” of 2:14-16 is not a perfume image. It is a Roman triumphal procession. See Section 6, item 2.
  • 2:16 asks a question the lesson does not answer: “And who is sufficient for these things?” Let it hang in the room for a few seconds. The answer does not arrive until 3:5-6, outside this week.

Quotes for this day

so overwhelming was Paul’s anxiety that he could not tarry to preach in Troas, even though the prospects there were bright. Here is evidence of his intense personal interest in his converts. We have no other record of Paul’s turning away from an open door. The most successful worker for God is not always above deep emotion that may unsettle him and render it impossible for him to continue his work for a time.

Use: the best pastoral material of the week. If there is a tired church officer in the room, this one sentence does more work than a whole devotional. Read it at 26–32.

the sun, which imparts life to a tree planted in good soil, decomposes and destroys it if it is plucked up and exposed on the surface of the ground. Sunlight melts wax but hardens clay. The difference is in the substances themselves. Even so with human hearts: some are melted, some hardened, depending on their response to the gospel.

Use: a good closing line for Thursday, and the answer to the third objection in Section 8.

“Titus finally arrives with good news, and Paul erupts: thanks be to God, which always causeth us to triumph in Christ. But notice, two verses earlier he wrote that he had no rest in his spirit. Weakness and triumph are on the same page.”

Spare question

“Paul walked away from an open door of ministry because he was anxious about one church. Was that the right call?”

5 The Two Questions Worth Real Time

minutes 6–16, the Tuesday block

“Paul spends two full chapters defending himself. Most of us were taught that a Christian should stay quiet and let God do the vindicating. Paul did not stay quiet. So is self-defense spiritual or not?”

Why it matters

The text forces this question and the lesson walks past it. Nearly every adult in your class has been spoken about unfairly in a church and has had to choose between explaining and staying silent, and some are still living with the consequences of that choice.

What to expect

Some will say self-defense is pride, and will cite Jesus silent before Pilate. Others will say that when the good name of a ministry is at stake, silence is irresponsible. Both have scriptural footing, and that is what makes it work.

How to keep it open

If someone closes it with “God knows the truth, that is enough,” ask the follow-up: “Then why did Paul not stop at that sentence? He wrote two chapters.” Then point them to 1:24 and ask what separates defending a ministry from defending your pride. Note that Paul never attacks anyone by name in return.

minutes 16–26, the Wednesday block

“This man hurt the church. The church rebuked him, and the rebuke worked. Now Paul tells them to confirm their love toward him. In our congregation, what would ‘confirm your love’ actually look like? Not the feeling, the action.”

Why it matters

This is the only place all week that demands something of the class rather than of Paul. And it is concrete: every congregation has such a person, and every adult in the room knows the name.

What to expect

“Speak to him again.” “Invite him for a meal.” “Give him his responsibilities back.” That last one is what will split the class, and that is the real discussion. Some will say forgiveness does not mean restoring office. That is a serious position and should not be brushed aside.

How to keep it open

The Sabbath School answer that will close this down is “we just have to forgive.” Reopen with: “Forgive in your heart, or hand him back the microphone? Paul did not write ‘just forgive.’ He wrote ‘confirm your love.’ What is the difference?” If the class hardens to one side, read SDABC on 2:6, which demands restoration and order at once. Do not resolve this argument; let them carry it out of the room.

Small groups

Four minutes in threes will noticeably raise the quality of the answers here, even though your class likes to talk, because the question concerns a real person and some members will not name him in front of everyone.

6 Background the Class Does Not Have

1. There is a textual question at 2 Corinthians 1:12

The KJV renders 1:12 “in simplicity and godly sincerity.” That follows the reading haplotēs (simplicity, singleness of heart). But a number of important early manuscripts read hagiotēs, that is, holiness.

This is not speculation. The Textus Receptus prints ἁπλότητι. Tischendorf and the SBLGNT print ἁγιότητι. NA28 prints ἁπλότητι with a variant marker. Both readings have real manuscript support.

Why this matters to you today. The official Teacher Comments for this week says Paul’s ministry was conducted with “holiness, sincerity, and transparency,” while Monday’s lesson builds its whole explanation on haplotēs, “simplicity.” A teacher with both documents open will be confused. Now you know why, and it is not a mistake by either writer.

How to use it in class. Do not open with this; it is not ten-minute material. Save it for the member who compares translations. One sentence is enough: both readings produce the same demand, purity of motive, and no doctrine rests on choosing between them.

2. The “savour” is a Roman triumph, not a perfume bottle

The verb in 2:14 is thriambeuō, used of the triumphal procession of a Roman general. Incense was burned along the route. The same smell reached two groups of people: the victors marching home, and the prisoners being led to execution. To the one it was the smell of victory; to the other, of death.

The Old Testament anointing incense (compare Exodus 30:22-33) marked people and places as belonging to God in the same way. (Official Teacher Comments, citing Andrews Bible Commentary 2022, p. 1665)

It changes how 2:15-16 reads. Without it, those verses sound like advice to be pleasant so that people are drawn to us. With it, the meaning is far harder: the same faithful life will attract some and repel others, and that is not a failure of method. Which is exactly why Paul immediately asks, “who is sufficient for these things?”

3. There are two ways for a church to lose, not one

In 5:5 Paul delivers an offender “unto Satan.” In 2:11 he warns that refusing to forgive lets Satan “get an advantage of us.” SDABC on 2:11 states the connection directly: Satan gains an advantage not only by leading people into sin, but also by our failure to forgive them when they repent.

This reframes the whole Wednesday discussion. Your class will divide into a “the church is too soft” camp and a “the church is too harsh” camp. Verse 11 says both camps are describing the same defeat from opposite sides.

Wednesday’s lesson notes that scholars differ on whether the man of 2 Corinthians 2 is the man of 1 Corinthians 5, and the lesson leans toward concluding it is someone else. SDABC on 2:11 connects the two. Two official Adventist sources on your desk disagree at this point. Say so plainly if anyone asks. The principle of discipline and restoration does not depend on the answer.

7 Ellen G. White for This Week

The assigned Friday reading: Ellen G. White, “The Message Heeded,” pp. 323–334, in The Acts of the Apostles. Open the table of contents. That is a link to the book, not to a specific page.

The quotation most worth reading aloud is not the Friday one. It is on Tuesday, from The Acts of the Apostles, pp. 300–301. The reason is simple: it answers the question the class will actually ask, which is why Paul did not just go.

The first Friday quotation

“Those who have borne the greatest sorrows are frequently the ones who carry the greatest comfort to others, bringing sunshine wherever they go. Such ones have been chastened and sweetened by their afflictions; they did not lose confidence in God when trouble assailed them, but clung closer to His protecting love. Such ones are living proof of the tender care of God, who makes the darkness as well as the light and chastens us for our good.”

God’s Amazing Grace, p. 122.3. Open this paragraph

Where to stop. The lesson cuts this after “the tender care of God.” The original paragraph continues with “who makes the darkness as well as the light and chastens us for our good,” and then moves into light and rejoicing. If you read from the lesson, you stop earlier than Ellen G. White did. For the class, stop at “for our good”; that clause carries the most weight and is the most likely to provoke a question.

The second Friday quotation

“A consecrated Christian life is ever shedding light and comfort and peace. It is characterized by purity, tact, simplicity, and usefulness.”

God’s Amazing Grace, p. 122.7. Open this paragraph

This applies to both Friday quotations above. God’s Amazing Grace is a devotional compilation, assembled after Ellen G. White’s death, not a book she wrote as a single work. Both paragraphs on page 122 are drawn from elsewhere: 122.3 comes from In Heavenly Places, p. 273, and 122.7 from Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 667. The pages and the wording are correct. But saying in front of the class “Ellen White wrote in God’s Amazing Grace” is imprecise. Say “Ellen G. White, quoted in God’s Amazing Grace, page 122.”

The third Friday quotation, and why it is special

“The apostle Paul found it necessary to reprove wrong in the church, but he did not lose his self-control in reproving error. He anxiously explains the reason of his action. How carefully he wrought so as to leave the impression that he was a friend of the erring! He made them understand that it cost him pain to give them pain.”

SDA Bible Commentary, vol. 6, p. 1094.1. Open this paragraph

What the lesson does not mention: in the source, this paragraph is headed “Paul a Friend of the Erring,” and immediately after that last sentence this week’s Memory Text, 2 Corinthians 2:4, is quoted in full. So this is not a general remark about Paul. It is Ellen G. White commenting on the Memory Text itself. It comes originally from Letter 16a, 1895. That makes it the right closing quotation for minutes 32–38.

8 Handling the Hard Spots

“If Paul really loved them, he would have come. That excuse sounds like self-justification.”

This is the objection most likely to come, and the person raising it is thinking clearly, not being difficult. Do not rush to defend Paul.

The answer from Scripture: Paul does not say he was unable to come. He says in 1:23 that he stayed away to spare them, and in 2:1 that he had determined not to come again in heaviness. So the visit was not a neutral act; the previous one had already been painful. Then turn it back to the class: “Have you ever decided not to go and see someone precisely because you knew the meeting would make things worse?” Almost every adult has.

“Church discipline is not loving. Jesus never shut anyone out.”

The answer is in Jesus himself. 18:17 gives a graded sequence that ends in the action of the church, and 18:18 gives the church authority to bind and loose. So discipline is not Paul’s invention. What makes it loving is its purpose, and the purpose is stated plainly in 2:7: lest he be swallowed up with overmuch sorrow. Discipline that does not end in restoration has failed, however correct the procedure. The full text is in Appendix A.

Read Matthew 18:15-18 tonight. This is the one thing you must prepare yourself, because if the objection above comes and you are not ready, the discussion will scatter and eat the Wednesday block.

“So if people reject our message, it is not our fault? That sounds like an excuse not to improve.”

This is a smart objection and should not be waved off, because it is partly right.

Notice that Paul puts the two things side by side. 2:15-16 says the same gospel is the savour of life to some and of death to others. But 2:17, the very next sentence, says there are those who corrupt the word of God. So Paul himself admits preaching can go bad. What he rejects is the conclusion that rejection always means our method was wrong. SDABC on 2:16 closes this well: the same sun melts wax and hardens clay.

Where the lesson is thin

On Wednesday, after treating 7:5-13, the lesson inserts a line about how important it is to support our leaders, and how church members can make their work much easier.

That is fine as advice, but it is not what the text says. What 7:9-10 says is that the church repented, and that godly sorrow works repentance unto salvation. If that line becomes Wednesday’s point, a passage about repentance turns into an appeal for loyalty to leadership. In a mixed adult class that can land as pressure, and somebody will notice.

Teach the text. Work 7:10 and distinguish godly sorrow from ordinary regret; the SDABC material for that verse, with the Peter and Judas contrast, is on the Wednesday card. Do not attack the quarterly in front of the class. Just teach the verse, and that line stays a marginal note rather than the point.

A Appendix A: Every Bible Text, KJV

A1. Memory Text and Key Text

A2. 2 Corinthians 1:3-14, to be read aloud

The opening that carries Sunday and Monday. Read straight down it without breaking for comment.

3 Blessed be God, even the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Father of mercies, and the God of all comfort;

4 Who comforteth us in all our tribulation, that we may be able to comfort them which are in any trouble, by the comfort wherewith we ourselves are comforted of God.

5 For as the sufferings of Christ abound in us, so our consolation also aboundeth by Christ.

6 And whether we be afflicted, it is for your consolation and salvation, which is effectual in the enduring of the same sufferings which we also suffer: or whether we be comforted, it is for your consolation and salvation. is effectual: or, is wrought

7 And our hope of you is stedfast, knowing, that as ye are partakers of the sufferings, so shall ye be also of the consolation.

8 For we would not, brethren, have you ignorant of our trouble which came to us in Asia, that we were pressed out of measure, above strength, insomuch that we despaired even of life:

9 But we had the sentence of death in ourselves, that we should not trust in ourselves, but in God which raiseth the dead: sentence: or, answer

10 Who delivered us from so great a death, and doth deliver: in whom we trust that he will yet deliver us;

11 Ye also helping together by prayer for us, that for the gift bestowed upon us by the means of many persons thanks may be given by many on our behalf.

12 For our rejoicing is this, the testimony of our conscience, that in simplicity and godly sincerity, not with fleshly wisdom, but by the grace of God, we have had our conversation in the world, and more abundantly to you-ward.

13 For we write none other things unto you, than what ye read or acknowledge; and I trust ye shall acknowledge even to the end;

14 As also ye have acknowledged us in part, that we are your rejoicing, even as ye also are ours in the day of the Lord Jesus.

Teaching note: watch the alternation in vv. 4-7. Afflicted, comforted, comforting. Paul does not offer a way out of suffering; he offers a direction for it. And v. 12 is the thesis of the letter, the sentence he will spend until chapter 7 proving.

A3. 2 Corinthians 1:15-24, for the Tuesday block

15 And in this confidence I was minded to come unto you before, that ye might have a second benefit; benefit: or, grace

16 And to pass by you into Macedonia, and to come again out of Macedonia unto you, and of you to be brought on my way toward Judaea.

17 When I therefore was thus minded, did I use lightness? or the things that I purpose, do I purpose according to the flesh, that with me there should be yea yea, and nay nay?

18 But as God is true, our word toward you was not yea and nay. word: or, preaching

19 For the Son of God, Jesus Christ, who was preached among you by us, even by me and Silvanus and Timotheus, was not yea and nay, but in him was yea.

20 For all the promises of God in him are yea, and in him Amen, unto the glory of God by us.

21 Now he which stablisheth us with you in Christ, and hath anointed us, is God;

22 Who hath also sealed us, and given the earnest of the Spirit in our hearts.

23 Moreover I call God for a record upon my soul, that to spare you I came not as yet unto Corinth.

24 Not for that we have dominion over your faith, but are helpers of your joy: for by faith ye stand.

Teaching note: the argument in vv. 18-20 is not “trust me.” It is trust the Christ I preached, because He was never yea and nay. Then in v. 24 he immediately pulls back his own authority. If one verse deserves reading twice in the Tuesday block, it is v. 24.

A4. 2 Corinthians 2:1-17, for the Wednesday and Thursday blocks

1 But I determined this with myself, that I would not come again to you in heaviness.

2 For if I make you sorry, who is he then that maketh me glad, but the same which is made sorry by me?

3 And I wrote this same unto you, lest, when I came, I should have sorrow from them of whom I ought to rejoice; having confidence in you all, that my joy is the joy of you all.

4 For out of much affliction and anguish of heart I wrote unto you with many tears; not that ye should be grieved, but that ye might know the love which I have more abundantly unto you.

5 But if any have caused grief, he hath not grieved me, but in part: that I may not overcharge you all.

6 Sufficient to such a man is this punishment, which was inflicted of many. punishment: or, censure

7 So that contrariwise ye ought rather to forgive him, and comfort him, lest perhaps such a one should be swallowed up with overmuch sorrow.

8 Wherefore I beseech you that ye would confirm your love toward him.

9 For to this end also did I write, that I might know the proof of you, whether ye be obedient in all things.

10 To whom ye forgive any thing, I forgive also: for if I forgave any thing, to whom I forgave it, for your sakes forgave I it in the person of Christ; person: or, sight in the person: or, in the sight

11 Lest Satan should get an advantage of us: for we are not ignorant of his devices.

12 Furthermore, when I came to Troas to preach Christ's gospel, and a door was opened unto me of the Lord,

13 I had no rest in my spirit, because I found not Titus my brother: but taking my leave of them, I went from thence into Macedonia.

14 Now thanks be unto God, which always causeth us to triumph in Christ, and maketh manifest the savour of his knowledge by us in every place.

15 For we are unto God a sweet savour of Christ, in them that are saved, and in them that perish:

16 To the one we are the savour of death unto death; and to the other the savour of life unto life. And who is sufficient for these things?

17 For we are not as many, which corrupt the word of God: but as of sincerity, but as of God, in the sight of God speak we in Christ. corrupt: or, deal deceitfully with in Christ: or, of Christ

Teaching note: this chapter moves from a very small room to a very large one. Verses 1-11 handle one man and one quarrel in one small congregation. Verses 14-16 open suddenly onto a triumphal procession and the whole world. The move is itself the sermon: how a church treats one offender is part of something far larger.

A5. 2 Corinthians 7:5-13, for the Wednesday block

5 For, when we were come into Macedonia, our flesh had no rest, but we were troubled on every side; without were fightings, within were fears.

6 Nevertheless God, that comforteth those that are cast down, comforted us by the coming of Titus;

7 And not by his coming only, but by the consolation wherewith he was comforted in you, when he told us your earnest desire, your mourning, your fervent mind toward me; so that I rejoiced the more.

8 For though I made you sorry with a letter, I do not repent, though I did repent: for I perceive that the same epistle hath made you sorry, though it were but for a season.

9 Now I rejoice, not that ye were made sorry, but that ye sorrowed to repentance: for ye were made sorry after a godly manner, that ye might receive damage by us in nothing. after: or, according to God

10 For godly sorrow worketh repentance to salvation not to be repented of: but the sorrow of the world worketh death.

11 For behold this selfsame thing, that ye sorrowed after a godly sort, what carefulness it wrought in you, yea, what clearing of yourselves, yea, what indignation, yea, what fear, yea, what vehement desire, yea, what zeal, yea, what revenge! In all things ye have approved yourselves to be clear in this matter.

12 Wherefore, though I wrote unto you, I did it not for his cause that had done the wrong, nor for his cause that suffered wrong, but that our care for you in the sight of God might appear unto you.

13 Therefore we were comforted in your comfort: yea, and exceedingly the more joyed we for the joy of Titus, because his spirit was refreshed by you all.

Teaching note: the list in v. 11 is worth pointing at with a finger. Seven items in a row, and not one of them is “feeling guilty.” They are all actions or changes of direction. That is what Paul means by godly sorrow, and it cuts short the assumption that repenting means feeling bad for long enough.

A7. Paul’s catalogue of suffering, 2 Corinthians 11:24-28

Useful if a member doubts that Paul has standing to speak about suffering.

24 Of the Jews five times received I forty stripes save one.

25 Thrice was I beaten with rods, once was I stoned, thrice I suffered shipwreck, a night and a day I have been in the deep;

26 In journeyings often, in perils of waters, in perils of robbers, in perils by mine own countrymen, in perils by the heathen, in perils in the city, in perils in the wilderness, in perils in the sea, in perils among false brethren;

27 In weariness and painfulness, in watchings often, in hunger and thirst, in fastings often, in cold and nakedness.

28 Beside those things that are without, that which cometh upon me daily, the care of all the churches.

Teaching note: the list ends in an unexpected place. After the whippings, the stoning and the shipwrecks, the last thing Paul names is the daily weight of caring for the churches. Not the most dramatic item, but the one he puts last.

A8. The verse cluster for the hard spots

The cluster most likely to be needed and least likely to be looked up. Printed in full.

Church discipline according to Jesus, Matthew 18:15-18

15 Moreover if thy brother shall trespass against thee, go and tell him his fault between thee and him alone: if he shall hear thee, thou hast gained thy brother.

16 But if he will not hear thee, then take with thee one or two more, that in the mouth of two or three witnesses every word may be established.

17 And if he shall neglect to hear them, tell it unto the church: but if he neglect to hear the church, let him be unto thee as an heathen man and a publican.

18 Verily I say unto you, Whatsoever ye shall bind on earth shall be bound in heaven: and whatsoever ye shall loose on earth shall be loosed in heaven.

The discipline case in the first letter, 1 Corinthians 5:1-5

1 It is reported commonly that there is fornication among you, and such fornication as is not so much as named among the Gentiles, that one should have his father's wife.

2 And ye are puffed up, and have not rather mourned, that he that hath done this deed might be taken away from among you.

3 For I verily, as absent in body, but present in spirit, have judged already, as though I were present, concerning him that hath so done this deed, judged: or, determined

4 In the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, when ye are gathered together, and my spirit, with the power of our Lord Jesus Christ,

5 To deliver such an one unto Satan for the destruction of the flesh, that the spirit may be saved in the day of the Lord Jesus.

Teaching note: notice that even in this, the harshest passage, the final purpose is stated in v. 5: that the spirit may be saved. That purpose is identical to 2 Corinthians 2:7. This is what joins the two passages, regardless of whether the man in view is the same.
